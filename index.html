01 / Software wasn't the plan yet

I thought I was going into sports medicine.

I went to school for sports medicine because, at the time, that was the career I saw for myself. I was not circling software or working toward engineering.

My life moved in a different direction and took me into restaurants and hospitality. That was not a calculated step toward technology. It was simply what came next.

02 / Learning operations under pressure

Then I learned how operations work when the pressure is real.

Before Chipotle, I ran a tap house and cigar bar. We closed the business at the start of the pandemic, and I went to Chipotle after that.

At Chipotle, I became a general manager and certified training manager (CTM), and I ran a training store. Staffing, training, customer expectations, speed, and quality all had to work at the same time. It was not enough for the process to work with an experienced team on a quiet day. We had to teach it clearly and make it hold up during a rush.

That was my real education in leadership and operations. I learned to listen to the people doing the work, communicate clearly, and pay attention to the handoffs where one missed detail created a much bigger problem.

That experience still affects how I evaluate software. If a tool only works when everything is calm and everyone remembers the process perfectly, it does not really work.

03 / The Minecraft rabbit hole

A game with friends is what made me fall in love with writing code.

I did not arrive at software through a formal career plan. I was playing Minecraft with friends and started experimenting with ComputerCraft. I wanted to understand how to write Lua programs for the turtles in the game.

I became obsessed with researching how it worked. I would read, try something, watch it fail, change the code, and run it again. Eventually I was writing programs the turtles could execute to gather materials and build things.

That feedback loop hooked me. I was not only using a system anymore. I could write a program, have a turtle execute it, and watch my code turn into something visible in the world. The more I learned, the more I wanted to know.

04 / Taking software seriously

That curiosity eventually led me to App Academy.

I did not come into software through a computer science degree. I came in because writing Lua for Minecraft made me realize how much I enjoyed solving problems with code. App Academy was the point where I decided to take that curiosity seriously and learn how to become a software engineer.

I had to build the engineering foundation from the ground up. That meant writing a lot of code, getting stuck, debugging things I did not understand yet, and learning how to ask better questions.

The early public projects below were not just portfolio pieces. They were where I learned the difference between code that runs and a product another person can actually use. I started caring as much about the flow, feedback, and tradeoffs as I did about getting the feature finished.

05 / Building around real workflows

I started building around real customer workflows.

Custom software was where the engineering work connected back to operations. Customers rarely arrived with a clean specification. They arrived with a spreadsheet, a workaround, a repeated frustration, or a process that depended too heavily on one person knowing how everything worked.

My job became more than implementing the request. I needed to understand what was getting stuck, which part should be simplified, and which details were specific for a good reason. That pulled me deeper into discovery, product design, client strategy, and architecture.

This was the point where my earlier experience stopped feeling unrelated. The hospitality background helped me hear the operational problem behind the feature request.

06 / Turning patterns into products

Now I am turning those lessons into products that can scale.

My current role takes the same kind of operational problems and asks a harder question: how do we build a SaaS product that works across customers without flattening the parts of their business that actually matter?

At Safe Right Fast Tech, I lead technology strategy and help build SaaS solutions for MEP contractors. That includes quality assurance, opportunity tracking, prefabrication, and manufacturing workflows. I work across client conversations, product direction, architecture, engineering leadership, and implementation.

I still want to stay close to the code because it keeps the strategy honest. I also want to stay close to the customer because technically clean software can still solve the wrong problem.

Good SaaS should preserve what is specific about the work without making every customer start from scratch.
